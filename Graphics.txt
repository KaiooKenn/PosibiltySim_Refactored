Based on your code, you have created an excellent interactive environment for setting up a probability
  experiment. You can define a population (the counts of different colors) and draw samples from it (filling
  the glasses).

  To add the "information part," you should focus on visualizing the core concepts of probability that your
  simulation demonstrates. Here are the mathematical graphics and displays you could design:

  1. The Probability Display Panel


  This would be a dedicated area on your screen (perhaps on the right side or top) that shows the core
  numbers in real-time. It doesn't have to be a complex graph, just clear text.


   * Theoretical Probability: For each color, calculate and display the probability based on the initial
     counts you set.
       * Formula: P(color) = (Initial count of that color) / (Total initial count of all colors)
       * Example Display:
           * P(Red): 0.50 (5/10)
           * P(Blue): 0.50 (5/10)


   * Experimental Probability: For each color, calculate and display the probability based on the glasses you
     have filled so far. This will change as you fill more glasses.
       * Formula: P(color) = (Number of glasses filled with that color) / (Total number of filled glasses)
       * Example Display:
           * Experimental P(Red): 0.45 (9/20)
           * Experimental P(Blue): 0.55 (11/20)


  2. The Bar Chart: Theoretical vs. Experimental

  A bar chart is a fantastic way to visually compare the expected results with the actual results.


   * Design: For each color, you would have two bars side-by-side:
       1. A bar representing the theoretical probability. This bar's height is fixed once you set the initial
          counts.
       2. A bar representing the experimental probability. This bar's height will grow and shrink as you fill
          more glasses.
   * What it Shows: The user can immediately see the difference between what should happen and what is
     happening.


  3. The Line Graph: The Law of Large Numbers

  This is the most powerful and educational graphic you can add. It perfectly demonstrates the Law of Large
  Numbers, which states that as the number of trials (filled glasses) increases, the experimental
  probability will get closer and closer to the theoretical probability.


   * Design:
       * X-Axis: Number of Trials (Total number of glasses filled).
       * Y-Axis: Probability (from 0.0 to 1.0).
       * Lines:
           1. For a chosen color (e.g., Red), draw a straight horizontal line at its theoretical probability
              (e.g., at Y=0.5). This line doesn't move.
           2. Draw another fluctuating line that plots the experimental probability of that color at each
              trial. After the 1st glass, the 2nd, the 3rd, and so on.
   * What it Shows: The user will see the experimental line jump around wildly at first, but as they fill more
      and more glasses, they will see it stabilize and converge towards the straight line of the theoretical
     probability. This is the core of many probability simulations.


  What to Do (Your Plan):


   1. Choose a Library: Your code already imports matplotlib.pyplot. This is the perfect tool for creating
      these graphs.
   2. Integrate Matplotlib with Pygame: You cannot directly show a Matplotlib plot in Pygame. The standard
      method is:
       * Create your plot (bar chart or line graph) using Matplotlib.
       * Instead of showing it (plt.show()), save it to an in-memory buffer (like io.BytesIO).
       * Load that buffer as an image into a Pygame surface (pygame.image.load).
       * Draw (blit) that surface onto your main game screen.
   3. Start Simple: Begin by implementing the text-based Probability Display Panel. It's the easiest to code
      and provides the essential data needed for the other graphs.
   4. Implement the Graphs: Once the text display is working, use that data to generate the bar and line
      charts. The line chart will be the most impressive addition.
